# The Difference Between A Good Engineer And A Trusted Consultant

Throughout my career, I've worked with some incredibly talented engineers.
People who could troubleshoot complex network issues, optimize challenging markets, and solve technical problems that seemed impossible at first.
Technical expertise is essential.
But over the years, I've realized something important.
Being a great engineer doesn't automatically make someone a trusted consultant.
The difference isn't technical knowledge.
It's perspective.
A good engineer focuses on solving the problem.
A trusted consultant focuses on solving the right problem.
That may sound like a small distinction, but it changes everything.

When a customer says, "We need better coverage," an engineer may immediately think about new sites or antenna optimization.
A consultant asks a different question:
"What business problem is poor coverage actually creating?"
Sometimes the issue isn't coverage at all.
It may be congestion.
Poor mobility.
Incorrect neighbor relationships.
Or simply unrealistic expectations based on how users behave.

The same applies to technology.
Customers don't invest in SON, SMO, Cloud RAN, or AI because they want new technology.
They invest because they expect measurable business outcomes.
Better customer experience.
Lower operational costs.
Faster service delivery.
Improved network efficiency.
That's why I believe consulting starts where engineering ends.
Engineering answers technical questions.
Consulting helps customers decide which questions are worth asking in the first place.

Another lesson I've learned is that trust is rarely built by always having the answer.
It's built by being honest when you don't.
Some of the strongest customer relationships I've developed came from saying:
"Let's validate the data before making a recommendation."
Or...
"There may be several possible causes. Let's eliminate them one by one."
Customers remember honesty long after they forget technical details.
As our industry moves toward AI-driven automation, this distinction becomes even more important.
AI can analyze data.
Automation can execute workflows.

But customers still need someone who understands technology, business priorities, operational constraints, and risk.
Someone who can translate complexity into confident decisions.
To me, that's the real role of a consultant.
Not to know everything.
But to help customers make better decisions with confidence.
Because in the end, customers rarely remember who solved the most technical problem.
They remember who helped them succeed.
